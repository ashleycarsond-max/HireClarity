Every job seeker asks the same question at some point, in one phrasing or another: is this job posting real? Is it legit? The posting is live, the apply button works, the application form accepts your resume. But will a human ever read it? It's a fair question in a market where postings routinely outlive the roles they describe — and where the answer isn't written anywhere on the posting itself.

## What "real" means

A posting can be two different things, and the difference matters. "Exists" means the posting is live somewhere on the internet. "Real" means there is an active hiring process behind it: someone is collecting applications, reviewing them, and planning to interview. A ghost job is a posting that exists but has no active process behind it — the role is filled, the budget is frozen, or the posting is being kept as an evergreen resume collector.

## Signs you can check yourself

You don't need any tools to gather the first few signals. Look at what the posting itself tells you:

- **Age.** If the board shows a posting date, that's your first clue. A posting listed for months isn't automatically fake, but it's older than most successful hiring cycles.
- **The company's career page.** Legitimate postings usually appear on the company's own careers page as well as on boards. If the role exists on third-party sites but not on the company site, treat it with suspicion.
- **Repetition.** Search for the same job title at the same company. If you see the same role posted multiple times with slightly different dates, the company may be recycling one posting rather than hiring.
- **The description itself.** Vague, template-sounding descriptions aren't proof of anything — many real postings are written by tired recruiters — but a posting with no specifics about team, location, or responsibilities gives you less to verify.

These signs are useful, but they have a ceiling. A fresh posting can be a ghost (the company hired internally and left the posting up). An old posting can be real (large companies run slow processes). Your own eyes can't settle the question; only continuous observation can.

## What the data shows

That's where tracking comes in. HireClarity Data watches roughly 8,000 live postings across about 90 companies and checks each one daily. Because we observe postings over time rather than once, we see things a single visit can't:

- **Listing duration.** How long a posting has been live, and how that compares with the company's other postings.
- **Take-down and relist cycles.** A posting that disappears and reappears with the same title and requirements is often the same role being recycled — a pattern strongly associated with postings that aren't attached to real hiring.
- **Board spread.** Which boards the posting appears on, and whether it is listed consistently across them.

These observations answer one narrow question — is there likely an active hiring process behind this posting — and they answer it from repeated observation, not a single snapshot. They don't tell you whether the company is good, whether the team is healthy, or whether you'd like the job. That's fine; those are separate questions.

We track postings on ATS-hosted boards (Greenhouse, Ashby, Lever, Workable) and public company career pages. LinkedIn and Indeed restrict automated access, so they are not part of our sample. If a posting lives only on LinkedIn or Indeed, we can't score it yet — and our coverage is always labeled as our observed sample rather than the whole market. ## A practical checklist

When you're staring at a promising posting, run this in order:

1. Note the posting date and the company's other postings. One old posting in an otherwise fresh board is different from an entire board that hasn't changed in months.
2. Check the company career page for the same role.
3. Watch for relist behavior if you've seen the posting before.
4. Run the URL through the free check tool and read the reasons.

And before you write a posting off, make sure you know what a ghost job actually is: /blog/what-are-ghost-jobs.

If the score comes back high, that doesn't mean "don't apply" — it means know what you're getting into, and don't make that posting your only application. The goal isn't to make you paranoid; it's to make your time count. Most postings lead somewhere. Knowing which ones probably don't is worth thirty seconds of checking before an hour of applying.
